Simulating Clean Neurons

Nick Cammarata argues neural networks are already clean. Leo Gao replies that clean-looking examples are not an explanation: you have to be able to simulate the neuron without breaking the model. This runs Leo's test on 278 random neurons of Qwen3-4B.

Qwen3-0.6B → 8BFineWeb, 2.1M tokensExplainer: Claude Opus 5.5Simulator: Claude Sonnet 5.5October 2026

Findings

  1. Weak activations matter in language models. Zeroing each neuron's weakest 75% of activations (Nick's ConvNeXt test) changes the top next-token prediction on 59% → 43% → 33% → 30% of tokens from 0.6B to 8B. Nick reports about 1 in 10 for ConvNeXt. Keeping only the "strong half" destroys every model.
  2. Explaining the strong firings perfectly is not enough. Swapping in a neuron's true activations wherever |a| ≥ 20% of its max, and zero elsewhere, avoids a median 28% of the damage of deleting it on ordinary text.
  3. Explanations that look right mostly don't work as stand-ins. Simulating each neuron from its Opus-written explanation avoids a median −1% of the damage on ordinary text, and 15% on windows where the neuron fires. A stronger simulator (Opus) does no better.
  4. Where the explanation works, it is a simple token detector. The neurons that simulate well are early- or last-layer detectors for a single word ("info", "afford", " un"). Neurons rated 5/5 "looks monosemantic" score 83% on active windows (n=7). Rated 3/5, they score 15%.

Background

The disagreement

Cammarata · Neural networks are already clean

Look harder and neurons have one job

  • Polysemanticity was mostly weak tools, weak models, and bad explanations.
  • What remains is generalization from a core concept, not superposition.
  • Qwen3-32B audit: about 90% of neuron poles have one main job.
  • ConvNeXt: removing the weakest 75% of activations costs 72% → 71% accuracy, so weak activations "usually don't matter."
  • Simulation scores are too demanding: they make you explain things that don't matter.
Gao · Neural networks are not already clean

Activating examples can fool you

  • Precision is not recall. Every top example fitting "dogs on bicycles" does not mean every dog on a bicycle fires the neuron.
  • Magnitudes matter. His guess: ablating weak activations substantially degrades an LM, which conflicts with Nick's result.
  • The test is simulation. Replace the neuron with a rubric-based simulation and measure downstream KL on a large dataset. KL forgives errors that don't affect behavior.
  • Neurons aren't circuits. Circuits between neurons stay large and redundant, which he reads as computation in superposition.

Leo's reply mostly says Nick's evidence was not tested hard enough. His first two points are things you can't see by reading examples. His third proposes a test that catches both. The experiments below run that test, and the weak-activation ablation he flagged as the place their results diverge.

Method

What was run

  1. Pick 278 random MLP neurons of Qwen3-4B, 28 in each of ten layers from 0 to 35. A neuron is one coordinate of the SwiGLU hidden state, so it has a positive and a negative pole.
  2. Record activations on 2.1M tokens of FineWeb. Claude Opus 5.5 sees about 60 windows (top, mid and weak examples of both poles, plus random ones) and writes an explanation with a scoring rubric. It also rates how monosemantic the neuron looks, from 1 to 5.
  3. Claude Sonnet 5.5 sees only the explanation and predicts every token's activation on 160 windows. These are 128 random windows plus 32 held-out windows where the neuron fires, shuffled together, and none was shown to the explainer.
  4. Substitute into the model: the neuron's value is replaced by the calibrated simulation, by the true activation kept only where it is strong, or by its mean. Then measure KL(clean ‖ patched) on every token.
  5. Re-weight windows to how often each kind occurs in real text, and score damage avoided = 1 − KL(substitute) / KL(mean). 100% is a perfect stand-in; 0% is no better than deleting the neuron.

Result 1

Weak activations are not free to drop

For every neuron in every layer at once, zero the weakest fraction of that neuron's activations, separately for each pole, and count how often the model's top next-token prediction changes.

At the 75% setting the 4B model loses only 3% of its activation energy, yet keeps just 67% of its top predictions (KL 0.48 nats). Next-token accuracy falls from 36% to 32%. A magnitude cutoff is harsher. Dropping everything below 5% of each neuron's max keeps only 19% of predictions, and keeping only the strong half keeps 0%. Larger models are more robust, but the gap to the vision result is large at every size tested.

This is not a clean replication of Nick's ConvNeXt experiment. Next-token prediction over a 151k vocabulary is a far more sensitive readout than 1000-way image classification, and the errors compound through 36 layers. It answers Leo's narrower question: in LMs, weak activations carry information the model uses.

Result 2

Explanations as stand-ins

Median damage avoided on ordinary web text, 278 neurons.

Two things stand out. First, even the oracle that knows every activation above 20% of max avoids only about a quarter of the damage on typical text. On the held-out windows where the neuron fires, the same oracle avoids 85%. Most of a neuron's effect on ordinary text comes from the small values below that line, which is Leo's magnitude point. Second, the explanation-based simulation sits near zero: only 4% of neurons get above 50%, against 23% for the 20% oracle. On active windows the simulation does better, a median of 15%, but it is far below the oracle.

The simulations are not noise. Their correlation with the true activation is a median 0.33 over the 160 eval windows. That is the old correlation-style score, and it would make these explanations look decent. Weighted to ordinary text the correlation drops to 0.10, and KL shows that most of what is right does not translate into the behavior the model needs.

Result 3

Looking clean versus being explained

The rating does carry signal. Neurons that look cleaner simulate better, which supports Nick's sense that examples are informative. But the top category is small: 7 of 278 neurons get a 5. A neuron rated 4/5, a coherent one-job story by the explainer's own judgment, still avoids only 5% of the damage on ordinary text.

The acronym neuron is the clearest case of Leo's point. On its strongest firings the simulation is nearly exact (correlation 0.94) and avoids most of the damage in those windows. On ordinary text it does worse than deleting the neuron, for two reasons. Even its true strong firings recover only 14% there, so most of its everyday effect is small values the explanation doesn't describe. And a score below zero means the simulation is putting wrong values into the model, most likely because "acronyms starting with C/P/D" is broader than what the neuron actually responds to. That second reason is inferred from the score, not checked token by token.

Checks

Is this the simulator's fault?

−1% / −1%Sonnet vs Opus as simulator, ordinary text, same 40 neurons
10% / 14%Sonnet vs Opus, held-out active windows
7% / 54%All 278 neurons patched at once: simulation vs 20% oracle (random windows)
  • Simulator strength. Re-simulating 40 neurons with Opus 5.5 at medium effort, in place of Sonnet at low effort, barely moves the score. The bottleneck is the explanation, not the actor.
  • Calibration. Simulator values are fit to activations with a per-neuron linear map, cross-fit on held-out halves. Without it, the raw −10..10 scale does far worse (median −74%), mostly from false positives.
  • Many at once. Patching all 278 sampled neurons together gives the same picture: the 20% oracle recovers 54% and the simulations 7%.
  • A bug caught on the way. The first simulator prompt marked tokens inline as ⟦i⟧token. The model often attached the index to the previous token, which shifted predictions one token late (correlation 0.49 at +1 versus 0.21 aligned). It was rerun with one token per line; alignment now peaks at shift 0 (0.41 versus 0.09 at ±1).

Limits

What this does not show

  • These are automated explanations from about 60 examples. Nick's audit used extended investigation per neuron. Better explanations could score higher, and the oracle rows bound how much higher they could go while only covering the strong firings.
  • One model, short context. Qwen3-4B with 64-token windows, which under-represents long-range features.
  • Mean ablation is the reference. Per-neuron KL effects are tiny in absolute terms (about 1e-6 to 1e-5 nats), so every score is relative to the same neuron's mean ablation. Everything runs in fp32 with TF32 off.
  • The E1 comparison to ConvNeXt crosses tasks and metrics, as noted above.

Questions worth asking Leo

  1. He mentions his own, not yet shared, results showing that ablating small activations hurts. Are they per-neuron or whole-model, and do they match the 0.6B→8B trend here?
  2. What fraction of the KL would he expect the strong firings to carry? Here a perfect 20% oracle gets about a quarter. Does he count the rest as "uninteresting but required" or as the real content?
  3. Would he accept a score that explains the small values with a generic rule, like "weak linear response to the residual direction"? Is that an explanation, or a reformulation?
  4. Which scoring would settle the disagreement for Nick: active-window KL, web-weighted KL, or something in between?
